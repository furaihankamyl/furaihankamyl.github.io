On 21 January 2020, LPKA Kelas II Jakarta moved out of a building it shared with an adult prison in Salemba and into its own facility in Cinere. LPKA is Lembaga Pembinaan Khusus Anak, Indonesia's special correctional institution for children. The move was meant to give the children a place built for them. My thesis asked what the move did to the organizations around them.

Those organizations matter because LPKA cannot run its programs alone. According to Direktorat Jenderal Pemasyarakatan, the number of children in LPKA across Indonesia grew from 823 in 2020 to 1,444 in 2024, a 75.4% increase in five years. Budgets, facilities, and staff did not grow at the same pace. The gap is filled by partners. Schools, NGOs, religious groups, the police, the civil registry, and ministries each run part of the program. **If the relocation disrupted that network, the children would feel it long before anyone measured it.**

## Turning a relocation into a comparison

The research question was simple. How did the effectiveness of LPKA's network governance change after the relocation?

A relocation is useful to a researcher because it is a single event with a date. Everything before 21 January 2020 is Salemba. Everything after is Cinere. That split gives a before and an after without anyone having to design an intervention.

The harder part was the data. No list of LPKA's partners existed. The official website published too few activities to build a representative network, which was the main weakness of the earlier PKM study I wrote about in [PIMNAS](article.html?slug=pimnas). So I scraped Instagram. The dataset covered 1,568 posts from @lpkajakarta (September 2017 to January 2025) and 221 posts from @lapassalemba (May 2017 to January 2020). I kept only posts that showed an in-person activity between organizations and dropped announcements. Two organizations appearing in the same activity became a tie. The result was a network of 210 organizations and 617 ties, cleaned in Python and analyzed in Gephi.

Network metrics describe structure. They do not explain it. I interviewed eleven informants from seven organizations, including Ditjenpas, the police, Disdukcapil Jakarta, Bapas Jakarta Selatan, an NGO, and an education partner, and spent a day observing activities in Cinere. The interviews were my check on whether the numbers meant what I thought they meant.

The analytical choice that shaped everything else was the benchmark. A dense, tightly connected network sounds healthy. Whether it is healthy depends on who is supposed to run it. Provan and Kenis (2008) describe three forms of network governance, and each one works under different conditions:

- Shared governance, where members coordinate among themselves. It works for small networks with high trust.
- Lead organization, where one member coordinates everyone else. It works with low density, high centralization, and a moderate number of members.
- Network administrative organization (NAO), where a separate body brokers the network. It works for larger networks that need higher competence.

So the order of the analysis mattered. I first identified which form LPKA's network had in each period. Then I judged each period against the conditions of its own form. Provan and Kenis name four conditions: trust, number of participants, goal consensus, and network-level competence. I translated each one into network metrics. Trust, for example, was read through density, centralization, and the average strength of ties.

## What changed after the move

The network became much larger and much more centralized.

In Salemba, the network had 41 organizations and a density of 0.133. The center was shared by three actors: LPKA, the regional office of the Ministry of Law and Human Rights (Kanwil Kemenkumham DKI Jakarta), and Lapas Salemba. Kanwil acted as the broker. Only 7 of Kanwil's 247 weighted ties in Salemba ran directly to LPKA. The rest connected Kanwil to everyone else. The interviews said the same thing. Disdukcapil Jakarta and Bapas Jakarta Selatan both described coordinating through Kanwil, not through LPKA. That is an NAO.

In Cinere, the network grew to 189 organizations. Degree centralization rose from 0.806 to 0.989, close to the maximum of 1. LPKA's own weighted degree reached 743. Of Kanwil's 356 weighted ties, 169 now ran to LPKA. Partners described LPKA setting the direction. Ruang Damai said LPKA's leadership gave direction on its program, including parenting sessions for the children's parents. PKBM Hasanah said its curriculum was designed on LPKA's input. That is a lead organization.

## Effective, with one mismatch

Judged against the conditions of a lead organization, the relocation was effective on three of the four conditions. Trust, goal consensus, and network-level competence all fit the new form.

Several results look backwards at first. Density fell by roughly three quarters, and that counts as effective. A lead organization is expected to run a sparse network in which most ties pass through itself. The same logic applies to the lower average tie strength.

The mismatch is size. A lead organization carries the coordination cost of every member on its own, so it works best with a moderate number of participants. LPKA's network grew from 41 to 189 organizations. Adjusted for the longer Cinere period, it still nearly doubled, from 0.056 to 0.106 organizations per day of data. It also became more diverse. Heterogeneity rose from 76.51% to 85.25%, with the largest growth among national agencies, law enforcement, correctional institutions, and NGOs. **LPKA took on the role of a lead organization without the capacity to lead a network this large and this varied.**

The recommendation follows from that finding. LPKA needs fewer partners, chosen more deliberately. Ditjenpas can set a framework for how LPKAs manage their networks, with criteria for selecting partners, a classification of which interactions take priority, and a simpler format for documenting cooperation. LPKA can then group its partners into thematic working groups by program focus, each with a small set of organizations whose resources complement each other.

The data has limits that anyone relying on it should know. Instagram only captures what LPKA and Lapas Salemba chose to publish, so unpublished interactions are missing. I also did not collect data from the children themselves. **The study measures how the network is governed. It does not measure whether the children are better off.** Answering that needs the children's perspective.

The question I would leave LPKA with is which of its 189 partners the children actually need.
