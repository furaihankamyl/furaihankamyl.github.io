In Universum Global's 2022 survey, arts and humanities students in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Singapore put public sector jobs at the bottom of their list. In Indonesia, Universum's 2017 survey found the opposite. Arts and humanities students most often wanted to become a civil servant, a PNS. Same career, opposite ends of the list.

History explains part of it. Officials held a special status under the pre-colonial kingdoms. The Dutch kept that status to run their administration. New Order patronage carried it forward. But we wanted to know what drives students now. So we asked what motivates students at Universitas Indonesia (UI) and Institut Teknologi Bandung (ITB) when they think about working for the government.

Adinda Puspita Ningrum led the study. Ricko Adi Wicaksana and I worked with her.

## Talking to eight students

A survey could tell us how many students want to be civil servants. It could not tell us why. So we interviewed four students from UI and four from ITB, from engineering, science, nursing, administration, and earth sciences. Each interview ran 25 to 40 minutes online. We kept adding people until we stopped hearing anything new.

Our questions followed the ten motivation factors of Berman et al. (2016), from competitive pay to putting the right person in the right job. The factors gave the conversation a shape, but students could bring up anything else. Several asked to stay anonymous, so every informant got a flower as a pseudonym.

We sorted the answers by the four work values of Ros (1999), plus a mixed category for answers that combine them.

Every student agreed that working in government lets you make an impact on society. Seven of eight felt their work would be appreciated by superiors, mostly in material form. Rules split them. Five of eight said the regulations are good. Seven of eight said the problem is how they are carried out. Pay was the weakest point. Only one student called it fair for the work.

**Six of eight would consider becoming a civil servant, but only as a last option, and only on conditions.** They wanted a public sector without corruption, collusion, and nepotism, better pay, and a better way of working.

**What attracts them is material.** Security came up most. Stable pay, a stable environment, and a clear career path. Family came second. Three students said their family encouraged them, and two of them have relatives in government. Both reasons sit in extrinsic and prestige values, which have the same material core.

What keeps them away is the workplace. Four of eight said it leaves too little room to create, innovate, and explore. One simply found the private sector more comfortable.

**Pay sits on both sides.** Students named stable income as the main reason to join. Three of eight also named it as a reason to hold back, because stable is still smaller than what the private sector or a job abroad pays. To us, that looks like a gap in what students know about civil service work after the reforms under Law No. 5 of 2014 on the civil service.

So stable pay already does its job. If government wants these students, the workplace is what needs fixing.

Eight students from two top universities in one province cannot tell us how common these views are. They can tell us why students hold them. We picked the two universities for their QS rankings and because both are in West Java.

The students want what the civil service promises. They doubt it can deliver.
