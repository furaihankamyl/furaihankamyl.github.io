In the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Singapore, arts and humanities students ranked public sector jobs at the bottom of their preferences in Universum Global's 2022 survey. In Indonesia, Universum's 2017 survey found that arts and humanities students most often preferred becoming a civil servant, a PNS. The same career sits at opposite ends of the list.

Part of the explanation is history. Public officials held a special status under the pre-colonial kingdoms, the Dutch colonial government kept that status to run its administration, and New Order patronage networks carried it forward. We wanted to know what drives students today. The question was simple. What motivational factors dominate how students at Universitas Indonesia (UI) and Institut Teknologi Bandung (ITB) see working in the public sector?

This study was led by Adinda Puspita Ningrum, with Ricko Adi Wicaksana and me.

## Listening to eight students

A survey could count how many students want to be civil servants. It could not explain why. We used in-depth interviews with four students from UI and four from ITB, from engineering, science, nursing, administration, and earth sciences. Interviews ran 25 to 40 minutes online. We kept adding informants until no new information came up.

Questions followed the ten motivation factors of Berman et al. (2016), from competitive pay to the right person for the job. The factors bounded the conversation but did not limit the answers, so other motivations could emerge. Several informants asked for anonymity, so every informant has a flower as a pseudonym.

We grouped the answers by four work values from Ros (1999), plus a mixed category that combines them.

Three patterns stand out. Every informant agreed that public sector work lets you make an impact on society, and seven of eight felt appreciation from superiors is fulfilled, though mostly in material form. Rules drew a split verdict. Five of eight called public sector regulations effective, but seven of eight said implementation is the problem. Pay was the weakest point, with only one informant calling remuneration commensurate.

The last row is the one that matters most for recruitment. 

<div class="callout"><span>Key finding</span>Six of eight would consider becoming a civil servant, but only as a last option, with conditions.</div>

 Those conditions were a public sector free of corruption, collusion, and nepotism, better remuneration, and a better working system.

**The factors that attract students are material.** The most frequently cited was security, meaning stable pay, a stable environment, and a clear career path. The second was family. Three informants named family encouragement, and two of them have family members working in government. Both factors tie to extrinsic and prestige values, which share a material character.

The factors that deter students are about the workplace. Four of eight named a workplace that is too restrictive for creating, innovating, and exploring. One found the private sector more comfortable.

**Pay appears on both sides.** Students named financial security as the main reason to join. Three of eight also named it as a reason to hold back, because stable pay is still smaller than what the private sector or work abroad offers. We read this as a gap in what students know about civil service work after the bureaucratic reforms under Law No. 5 of 2014 on the civil service.

For government, the finding points past pay stability, which already draws students in. What holds them back is the workplace.

The study reflects eight students from two top universities in one province, so it describes motivations in depth. It cannot say how common they are. The universities were chosen for their QS rankings and shared location in West Java.

The students want what the civil service promises and doubt it can deliver in practice.
