Reporting a broken streetlight in Jakarta takes a photo, a location, and a few taps in JakLapor, the complaint feature inside the JAKI app. It also takes personal data. JakLapor asks for a phone number, a NIK, and the user's location. Between May 2020 and July 2021, Indonesia saw leaks of voter list data, BPJS Kesehatan data, and data from the Ministry of Health's e-HAC app. So we asked a simple question. After those leaks, do young people in Jakarta still trust a government app with their data?

Young people were the group that mattered most. They are the most digitally literate and the most active users of e-government apps. They are also the most careful about sharing personal information online. No earlier study had compared trust in JakLapor across groups of young users.

## How we measured trust

We surveyed young people across Jakarta in August 2023. Kepulauan Seribu was left out because it has the smallest young population, 15,512 people across Gen Z and Millennials, and because of our time and budget. The Lemeshow formula set a minimum sample of 267. We collected 353.

Trust followed the framework of Hu et al. (2019), with two dimensions. Trust of government asks whether users trust the institution behind the app. Trust of platform asks whether they trust the app itself. Each dimension had three items. Before reading any result, we checked that the instrument held up. Every item correlated significantly with its dimension, and all reliability scores passed the .600 threshold.

## What we found

**Most young users trust JakLapor at a moderate level.** Of 353 respondents, 243 fell in the moderate category, 106 in high, and 4 in low. Moderate was also the largest category in every group we looked at.

Two details keep this finding in proportion. The correlation between generation and trust is weak (rs = .122), so generation explains only a small part of the variation. Gender came close to significance (p = .075) but did not cross the .05 line, so we did not report it as a difference.

## What the city can do

The literature points to two levers. Banghui et al. (2020) found that younger people are more willing to use e-government services when government social media content matches what they need. Alomari (2022), in a study of 510 young people in Jordan, found that social influence and word of mouth drive young people's trust in mobile government.

We turned both into two steps the DKI Jakarta Provincial Government could take directly:

Survey users directly</td><td>A needs survey inside JakLapor and on government social media, asking what information users need, what services they need, and why they use the app</td></tr><tr><td>2. Publish more of what they asked for</td><td>Increase the amount of service content on JakLapor and government social media that matches the survey results</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

The study has limits. The sample was drawn by accidental, non-probability sampling, so it describes the respondents we reached. It does not represent all young Jakartans. It leans toward women (60.91%) and Gen Z (66.57%). The survey also measured trust at one point in time.

The same survey also measured whether young people intend to engage with JakLapor at all, which is the subject of [the companion paper](research/jaklapor-engagement/).

The youngest users are the most active on e-government apps and the least convinced by this one.
