Reporting a broken streetlight in Jakarta takes a photo, a location, and a few taps in JakLapor, the complaint feature inside the JAKI app. It also takes your phone number, your NIK, and your location. Between May 2020 and July 2021, Indonesia leaked voter list data, BPJS Kesehatan data, and data from the Ministry of Health's e-HAC app. So Bunga, Gavrel, Wahyu, and I asked a simple question. After all that, do young people in Jakarta still trust a government app with their data?

We focused on young people for a reason. They use e-government apps the most. They are also the most careful about what they share online. And nobody had compared their trust in JakLapor across groups before.

## How we measured it

We ran the survey across Jakarta in August 2023. We left out Kepulauan Seribu. It has the smallest young population, 15,512 people across Gen Z and Millennials, and we had limited time and money. The Lemeshow formula said we needed at least 267 respondents. We got 353.

We measured trust with the framework of Hu et al. (2019). It has two parts. Do you trust the government behind the app? And do you trust the app itself? Each part had three questions. Before looking at any result, we checked the questionnaire. Every item correlated with its dimension, and every reliability score passed .600.

## What we found

**Most young users trust JakLapor, but only moderately.** Of 353 respondents, 243 were moderate, 106 high, and 4 low. Moderate was the biggest category in every group.

Only one difference held. **Millennials trust JakLapor more than Gen Z (p = .022).** Both generations had the same median score, 18. The difference was how many sat above it. 37.29% of Millennials did. 26.38% of Gen Z did.

I would not oversell it. The correlation is weak (rs = .122), so generation explains only a small part of why trust varies. Gender came close (p = .075), but close is not significant, so we did not report it as a difference.

## What the city can do

Two earlier studies pointed the way. Banghui et al. (2020) found that young people use e-government services more when government social media posts match what they need. Alomari (2022) studied 510 young people in Jordan and found that their trust in mobile government runs on social influence and word of mouth.

So we suggested two steps the DKI Jakarta Provincial Government could take on its own:

<div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What it involves</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1. Survey users directly</td><td>A needs survey inside JakLapor and on government social media, asking what information users need, what services they need, and why they use the app</td></tr><tr><td>2. Publish more of what they asked for</td><td>Increase the amount of service content on JakLapor and government social media that matches the survey results</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

Our sample has limits. We used accidental sampling, so the results describe the people we reached, not every young Jakartan. The sample leans toward women (60.91%) and Gen Z (66.57%). And trust was measured once, at one point in time.

The same survey also asked whether young people intend to use JakLapor at all. That is [the companion paper](research/jaklapor-engagement/).

The youngest users are the most active on e-government apps, and the least convinced by this one.
